It makes you wonder why some patterns in humanity repeat so many times. In some ways we are a species with a handful of core desires and archetypes that we keep acting out, over and over. You can find a story from several thousand years ago and then find the same story playing out in your own neighbourhood. You may know people who have lived it themselves -- the same pain, the same loss, the same mistakes, repeated as if nobody had ever made them before. It never seems to end.

Yesterday I wrote about how much of humanity is built on stories -- the ones we tell the next generation, our sons and daughters, so that something of us is left behind. When we raise children we accept that we will lose sight of most of their lives. We cannot be there for every decision they make. All we can do is hope they learned what they needed to live a good life, a kind life, a just life, and that they will not abandon their morals when tragedy comes or when hope runs out, but hold on to what they were handed.

<Claude re="how much of humanity is built on stories">
The Qur'an ends the story of Yusuf, the longest single story it tells, with exactly this claim: *"There was certainly in their stories a lesson for those of understanding"* (12:111). And the first paragraph above has an old companion in Ecclesiastes: *"The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be; and that which is done is that which shall be done: and there is no new thing under the sun"* (1:9). Both treat the repetition as the reason to tell the stories, not a reason to give up on them.
</Claude>

But what if we fail to tell them? What if we never tell our children the life we lived, the problems we faced, the things we got through? How do we teach them that a mistake can be made once, and should not be made twice? And what if there is no voice left to say it, and that space gets filled instead by people who live their own lives half-heartedly? How do you take advice from people who are still "kache" themselves -- raw, like clay that was never put in the fire -- and who never tried to become "pakka"? A kacha pot holds water for a while. Then it gives way.

And a pot only becomes pakka one way: it goes into the fire. I think people are the same. The ones whose word you can lean on are usually the ones who went through something and did not run from it -- a loss, a failure, a long stretch of carrying more than they wanted to. Nobody becomes pakka by being kept away from every heat. It is a strange thing to wish for your children, but I think it is true: the hope is not that they never meet the fire, but that someone is standing near them when they do.

We should not love half-hearted lovers, and we should not give our hearts to people who only ever give half of theirs.

For young men and boys, the worry every generation has seen is that the male psyche is left unexplored, or made to feel unwanted. So we have men who are lost and confused even after many years -- unable to let go of the grief of the past, unable to say what they think, unable to express what feels natural to them instead of what social pressure tells them to say, unable to find out who they are and what they want, unable to make decisions for themselves, unable to think critically about what they want to make of their lives. You hardly find a young boy who understands that there is a life after tomorrow, one he will have to live and should be preparing for now. Life is far more than games and entertainment. It is responsibility, handed down as a first principle: being someone who takes care of others, who does the things that make life better for the family around him.

<Claude re="unable to find out who they are and what they want">
Iqbal spent much of his poetry on this young man. His word for what is missing is *khudi*, selfhood: not ego, but a self that has been built and owned. The best-known couplet, from Bal-e-Jibril:

*Khudi ko kar buland itna ke har taqdeer se pehle / Khuda bande se khud pooche, bata teri raza kya hai.*

Raise your selfhood so high that, before every decree of fate, God Himself asks His servant: tell me, what is your will? It is a demanding picture of a young man. The self is not handed to him; he has to build it, and then answer for it.
</Claude>

And I think the two worries are the same worry. A boy who was never told the stories, who has no pakka voice anywhere near him, does not grow out of this on his own. He fills the silence with whatever is loudest, and what is loudest is rarely the voice of someone who has carried a family.

And I don't think that voice has to be a perfect one. Perhaps the most useful thing an older man can tell a boy is about the times he got it wrong -- the decision he regrets, the year he wasted, the person he should not have trusted, the thing he wanted badly and was lucky not to get. A father who only gives his son the rules gives him a list. A father who tells him where he himself fell gives him a map.

... Anyways, these were not the thoughts I started with. I had been worrying about something else these past couple of days.

There is a kind of man every generation seems to produce, and we all know his story. He starts out decent. He has a family, and he means it when he tells you what is right and what is wrong. Then he rises -- money, power, a name, rooms full of people who want something from him -- and somewhere along the way he does the very thing he always said he would never do. He betrays the wife who stood by him before any of it, and the children who trusted him. If you had asked him twenty years earlier, he would have condemned it as loudly as anyone.

<Claude re="There is a kind of man every generation seems to produce">
Shakespeare wrote a whole sonnet about this man, and about the one who refuses to become him. Sonnet 94 opens with the ones who hold back:

*They that have power to hurt and will do none, / That do not do the thing they most do show, / Who, moving others, are themselves as stone, / Unmoved, cold, and to temptation slow, / They rightly do inherit heaven's graces*

and closes on the ones who don't: *"For sweetest things turn sourest by their deeds; / Lilies that fester smell far worse than weeds."* The point of the last line is the one in this paragraph. A man who was admired, who stood for something, smells worse when he rots than one who never claimed much at all.
</Claude>

What makes a man do that? Something he would always have called wrong in public, never hinting that he quietly wanted the thing he was condemning? What is it in men that makes us say one thing and do the other, again and again?

I think part of the answer is stories again -- the ones a man tells himself. Nobody wakes up one morning and decides to betray his family. He decides something much smaller, and then tells himself a story about why it is fine. The pressure he is under. How nobody at home understands what his days are really like. How he has earned a little space. How this time is different. Each story is reasonable on its own, and each one makes the next step look like the same step as the last. By the end he has talked himself into a place he would have refused to stand in at the beginning, and every sentence of it made sense at the time. The same gift that lets us hand lessons down to our children lets us talk ourselves out of them.

And the part that actually frightens me is not him. It is that he could be me. <Gloss on="He could be any of us.">Solzhenitsyn, who had every reason to divide the world into guards and prisoners, refused to: “If only there were evil people somewhere insidiously committing evil deeds, and it were necessary only to separate them from the rest of us and destroy them. But the line dividing good and evil cuts through the heart of every human being.” (The Gulag Archipelago).</Gloss> Perhaps he could be most men, <Gloss on="given the right room and enough years in it">Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil (§146): “Wer mit Ungeheuern kämpft, mag zusehn, dass er nicht dabei zum Ungeheuer wird. Und wenn du lange in einen Abgrund blickst, blickt der Abgrund auch in dich hinein.” He who fights with monsters should see to it that he does not become a monster himself. And if you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss also gazes into you. Long is the word that matters.</Gloss>. I even suspect it is not only men -- that this is something power does to whoever holds it, and men meet it more often because more men have held it. That does not comfort me. If it were a flaw in a certain kind of man, I could check whether I am that kind. If it is something power does to people, nobody gets to be sure in advance.

<Claude re="I even suspect it is not only men">
There is research on exactly this suspicion. Lammers and colleagues (2011) surveyed about 1,500 professionals and found that the more power people held at work, the more likely they were to have broken a partner's trust, or to say they would, and the link held for women as much as for men. It ran through confidence: power made people surer they could attract someone. One of the co-authors was later found to have fabricated data in other studies; this paper was not retracted, and the university's review did not list it as fraudulent, but it is worth knowing.

A separate line of work points at what power does to attention. Hogeveen, Inzlicht and Obhi (2014) found that people who had just been made to feel powerful showed less motor resonance, the brain's quiet tendency to mirror the actions of the person you are watching, which is one rough sign of tuning in to someone else. Both are about power changing people in general, which is what makes the fear in this paragraph reasonable rather than gloomy.
</Claude>

Some things are bad regardless of the circumstances around them. Two and two never make five, and I don't think there is any larger philosophy or world view, in any shape or form, that leads you to conclude that betraying your wife and children is good. No decent person would argue for it, and no philosophy that takes human dignity seriously would call it "not bad".

There are layers to wrongdoing, and I accept that. You might lie to protect someone's dignity in a moment where the truth would do no justice and help no one, and perhaps you could defend that lie afterwards. But you cannot cover up something like this and then decide that the covering up was the good part. Some things do not get better for being hidden.

So there are two layers here: what shapes an action, and the one who acts it. The second is simple. The man is responsible, permanently, for what he did and for the hurt it caused his family. No room he sat in did it for him. Men will agree with that; there is no doubt in it.

The first layer is harder, because it points at the rest of us. <Gloss on="The circles you sit in">The proverb is older than all of us. Cervantes has it in Don Quixote, “Dime con quién andas, y te diré quién eres” — tell me who you walk with, and I will tell you who you are — and Goethe later put his own version of it among his Maxims and Reflections.</Gloss>, the places you go, and how easily your actions get excused there are all shaped by <Gloss on="the company you choose to keep">The Prophet ﷺ put it plainly: “A man is upon the religion of his close friend, so let each of you look at whom he befriends” (Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi). And the image of the perfume seller and the blacksmith (Bukhari, Muslim): sit with the first and you will at least come away smelling of musk; sit at the bellows of the second and at the very least you leave with the smell of smoke, if he does not burn your clothes.</Gloss>. That excuses nothing. But it does mean a man has to be careful about who he sits with, and where. And yet we men rarely find the courage to stand up and leave the rooms where relationships are treated as transactions, and where family is talked about as the thing that "holds you back". We stay, we laugh along, and we tell ourselves it will not touch us.

But it does touch us. A room rarely forces anything on a man. What it changes is what feels normal. Around money, power and people who want something from you, the things that used to stop you wear down quietly. There is no single bad decision. The guardrails just thin, a little at a time, until one day they are not there. And the higher a man goes, the more the circle around him is chosen by other people's interests instead of his own. If he does not decide who gets close to him and where he spends his evenings, someone else will decide it for him.

<Claude re="The guardrails just thin">
C. S. Lewis gave this to his senior devil in The Screwtape Letters, advising a junior tempter not to bother with dramatic sins: *"Indeed the safest road to Hell is the gradual one — the gentle slope, soft underfoot, without sudden turnings, without milestones, without signposts"* (Letter XII). The Qur'an uses the same picture of steps: *"O you who have believed, do not follow the footsteps of Satan"* (24:21). Footsteps, not a leap. Nobody is asked to jump; only to take the next step, which looks a lot like the last one.
</Claude>

Men seem to forget that the standard they will be judged on never changes, <Gloss on="however high they climb">Montaigne, in the last of his essays (“Of Experience”, III.13): “It does us no good to mount on stilts, for on stilts we must still walk with our own legs; and on the loftiest throne in the world we are still sitting on our own behind.” The height changes; the man sitting there does not.</Gloss> -- and society would quite like them to forget it, for its own reasons. A man who believes success has earned him a looser standard is a man who is easier to flatter, to use and to sell to. It really runs the other way. The higher you go, the less room there is for a "chhoti si galti".

Leaving the room is not dramatic, most of the time. It is going home while the evening is still going. It is not laughing at the joke about someone's wife. It is saying no to the trip that does not need you, and to the dinner where everyone knows what happens after the dinner. And it costs something. You become the dull one, the one who does not quite belong, and sometimes the one who is not invited next time, along with whatever came with the invitation. I think it is worth being honest that a guardrail has a price. If it cost nothing, every man would already have one.

And I think there is something under all of this, which takes me back to the boys I was worrying about at the start. The boy who could never say what he thought does not grow out of it just because he grows up. He becomes a man who cannot say "I am struggling", or "I am tempted", or "I am lonely in my own home", to anyone at all. <Gloss on="So he carries it alone">Thoreau, in 1854: “The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation” (Walden, “Economy”). The quiet is the part that has not changed.</Gloss>, and it grows in the dark, until the day he acts on it. I don't think most men fall because nobody warned them. They fall because there was no one they could tell the true thing to while it was still small. The silence a boy grows up in is the same silence the man falls in, years later.

Which means the answer to the silence is not only for the man to speak. It is for someone to be the kind of person he could speak to -- a brother, a friend, an older man who can hear "I am tempted" without laughing and without recoiling, and then tell him the truth anyway. I would like to be that for someone, and I think it is harder than it sounds. It means being trusted with the small true thing before it becomes the big one, and not using it against him.

It is as if wealth and power, of the most immense kind, are a different kind of sickness. One that looks like a gift, but isn't. <Gloss on="Lord Acton's old line">From a letter to Bishop Mandell Creighton, 5 April 1887: “Power tends to corrupt and absolute power corrupts absolutely.” The paragraph it sits in argues that popes and kings should be judged by the same moral standard as everyone else, and not given the benefit of the doubt because of their office — close to what this post says about the measure not moving with the room.</Gloss> was that power *tends* to corrupt, and I think the word people forget is the important one. It is not a sentence passed on every man who rises. It is a pull, and a pull can be leaned against -- but only by someone who admits it is there.

My own standard here is harsh, and I mean it. I judge men heavily who cheat on their families and hurt them. Men at the top who do this should be set aside, not admired. We cannot, as men, let the idea grow that this is excusable, or something to be quietly pardoned because the man is rich or brilliant or important.

But I have to be careful to keep two things apart, because they blur easily. Excusing an act is one thing, and pardoning a person is another. I can hold, without compromise, that betraying your family is never excusable, and still accept that forgiveness belongs first to the wife and children who were wronged, and not to the rest of us watching. Our part is to refuse to call the act normal. It is not our place to rule on a man's whole life. And a standard that only applies to the men who get caught, or to the ones we already dislike, is not really a standard. It is a mood.

The wife in that story knew him before any of it -- before the money, before the name, before the rooms. She is one of the few people in his life who wanted nothing from what he became. The people who knew you before the gift are the ones who love you without it. When a man trades them for the people who arrived with the gift, he trades the ones who were there for him for the ones who are there for it.

The strict standard has the most force inside my own home. Judging other men costs me nothing. The real test, if it comes, will come quietly, years from now: a tired marriage, a long night far from home, someone who flatters me at exactly the right moment. If I hold the line, I have to hold it over myself first.

Which brings me back to the fear I started with.

*Shayad waqai mein woh insaan itna shareef nahi jisko kabhi moqa nahi mila.*

Perhaps the man who was never given the chance is not as decent as he thinks he is. What can you really ask of someone who never did wrong on purpose, when wrong never came knocking at his door? Honesty that was never tested is only a claim. Real decency shows when there is the chance, and the power, and nobody watching, and the man still stops. This has become a different understanding for me. The sin is not only the thing you point at in someone else. It is also believing that wherever you end up in life, however high, the standard inside you will hold on its own, without you holding it.

<Claude re="the man who was never given the chance">
Plato put this exact test into the Republic, about 2,400 years ago. Glaucon tells the story of Gyges, a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, and uses it to slip into the palace, kill the king and take the throne. Then he asks what would happen if a just man and an unjust man each had such a ring: *"no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice. No man would keep his hands off what was not his own"* (Book II, Jowett's translation). His challenge is the one in this paragraph: is anyone good, or only unobserved?

Socrates spends the rest of the book answering him, and his answer, at the very end, is that the man who uses the ring has not won anything. He has made himself a slave to his own appetites.
</Claude>

I don't want this to turn into thinking that every man is simply waiting for his chance. That is cynicism, and it is its own trap. Not getting the chance and not giving yourself the chance are different things, and the second one is discipline. The man who keeps certain doors shut around himself on purpose is not weak; he is wise. And if the measure inside a man loosens as the role, the money and the praise grow, then it was never a measure. It was only his circumstances.

<Claude re="keeps certain doors shut around himself on purpose">
The finest picture of this I know is in The Fellowship of the Ring. Frodo offers the Ring to Gandalf, the wisest and most powerful person he knows, and Gandalf refuses at once: *"Do not tempt me! For I do not wish to become like the Dark Lord himself. Yet the way of the Ring to my heart is by pity, pity for weakness and the desire of strength to do good."* He doesn't refuse because he thinks he is too good to be corrupted. He refuses because he knows exactly how it would happen to him, and through which of his virtues. That is the doors-shut discipline, at its wisest.
</Claude>

But there is a feeling I cannot shake, and I think most of us carry it without saying so. A man cannot fully trust even himself. One thing yesterday, another today. <Gloss on="When I am calm, I am a poor judge of who I will be in the moment I want something.">Psychologists call this the hot–cold empathy gap (George Loewenstein): in a calm state we underestimate how much desire, anger or hunger will change what we do. Experiments on it keep finding the same thing: people asked the same questions once calm and once in the grip of a strong want answer very differently, and neither version of them predicts the other well. Economists call the mast in the next paragraph a commitment device: a choice made in advance that takes the later choice away.</Gloss> We are the ones who warn against the sin, and we are the ones who commit it.

<Claude re="One thing yesterday, another today">
European literature has been circling this sentence for centuries. Goethe's Faust says it in two lines that every German schoolchild knows: *"Zwei Seelen wohnen, ach! in meiner Brust, / Die eine will sich von der andern trennen"* — two souls, alas, live in my breast, and each one wants to tear itself away from the other.

Montaigne, who watched himself more honestly than almost anyone, opens his essay on the inconstancy of our actions by saying we are *"all patchwork, and so shapeless and diverse in composition that each bit, each moment, plays its own game"* (Essays II.1). And Kant, the most systematic moralist of them all, still admitted: *"Aus so krummem Holze, als woraus der Mensch gemacht ist, kann nichts ganz Gerades gezimmert werden"* — out of timber as crooked as that from which man is made, nothing entirely straight can be built (1784). None of the three took this as a reason to stop trying. They took it as the reason the trying never ends.
</Claude>

I don't think that makes us hypocrites, though. Warning against a sin and then committing it is a contradiction in human nature. Hypocrisy is something else: it is when a man falls and then lowers the standard so that the fall no longer looks like one. And admitting that I cannot fully trust myself is not weakness either. The man who says "I could never slip" <Gloss on="is the one in the most danger">Pascal, Pensées: “L'homme n'est ni ange ni bête, et le malheur veut que qui veut faire l'ange fait la bête.” Man is neither angel nor beast, and the trouble is that whoever tries to play the angel ends up playing the beast.</Gloss>.

<Claude re="a contradiction in human nature">
This is one of the oldest confessions in writing. Ovid gives it to Medea: *"video meliora proboque, deteriora sequor"* — I see the better way and approve it; I follow the worse (Metamorphoses VII). Paul writes it about himself: *"For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do"* (Romans 7:19).

And La Rochefoucauld has a line that fits the distinction above almost too well: *"Hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue"* (Maxim 218). Even the hypocrite still admits the standard exists; he hides from it, which is a kind of respect. The man who moves the measure has stopped paying even that.
</Claude>

So if I cannot fully trust myself, the answer is not to hate myself for it. The answer is structure. <Gloss on="Keep away from the openings.">The Qur'an words many of its warnings this way. The very first one, to Adam in the garden, is not “do not eat” but “do not approach this tree” (2:35). And of the limits it sets elsewhere: “These are the limits of Allah, so do not approach them” (2:187). Not “do not cross”, but “do not come near” — the guardrail is built into the wording.</Gloss> Make the decisions while my head is clear, instead of in the moment the wanting is speaking -- the way Ulysses had himself tied to the mast before he ever heard the Sirens. And keep people close who will tell me the truth, which for a lot of men is harder than it sounds, because those friendships quietly ended with school, or a team, or a job, and were never rebuilt. Building them again is part of the guardrail. I may not be able to trust who I will be tomorrow, but I can trust the decisions I make today to protect him. Let the feeling keep me alert, not hopeless.

<Claude re="those friendships quietly ended">
There is a number behind this. In a 2021 American survey by the Survey Center on American Life, 15% of men said they had no close friends, up from 3% in 1990, and the share with six or more close friends fell from 55% to 27%. Women's numbers fell less. It is one country, and it measures what people report rather than what their friendships are actually like, but the direction is hard to miss. The usual explanation in that research matches the sentence above: men's friendships tend to be built around a shared place or activity, and when the place goes, the friendship often goes with it.
</Claude>

It made me think of tawbah. We usually translate it as repentance, but the word itself means to return. It has a direction in it: you went somewhere you should not have gone, <Gloss on="and you come back">Victor Hugo built Les Misérables on this return. When the bishop lets Jean Valjean keep the silver he stole, and adds the candlesticks, he tells him: “Jean Valjean, mon frère, vous n'appartenez plus au mal, mais au bien” — my brother, you no longer belong to evil, but to good. The rest of the book, well over a thousand pages of it, is what keeping that return costs him.</Gloss> -- to the same place, the same standard, and not to a new and lower one you built so that the fall would not look like a fall. And one of the names of Allah, at-Tawwab, comes from the same root: the One who keeps turning back to the one who returns.

<Gloss on="The scholars set out what that return asks">The best-known statement is Imam an-Nawawi's, in the chapter on repentance in Riyad as-Salihin: three conditions when the sin is between a person and God, and a fourth when it concerns another person's right, which must be restored, or their pardon sought.</Gloss>: leave the sin, regret it, and resolve not to go back to it. And when the sin wronged another person, a fourth thing -- their right has to be restored, and their pardon sought. So a man who betrayed his family cannot settle it between himself and God alone and walk past the people he hurt. Part of the way back runs through them.

<Gloss on="Most men will fall somewhere in their lives">“Every son of Adam sins, and the best of those who sin are those who repent” (Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah). And the verse often called the most hopeful in the Qur'an: “Say, O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins” (39:53).</Gloss>, in something smaller or larger. The man who comes back, ashamed, to the measure he left has fallen. The man who moves the measure to meet himself has done something worse, because now there is nothing left to return to.

So, back to the gift. Wealth and power arrive looking like a reward, and they arrive with the friction taken out -- fewer people who say no, more doors left open, more voices telling you that you have earned an exception. Whatever a man has not built inside himself before then, they will not build for him.

<Claude re="looking like a reward">
The title has a famous ancestor. In the Aeneid, when the Trojans find the wooden horse outside their walls, the priest Laocoön warns them: *"timeo Danaos et dona ferentes"* — I fear the Greeks, even bearing gifts (II.49). They take it in anyway, because it looks like a gift.

The Qur'an names wealth in the same way: *"And know that your properties and your children are but a trial, and that Allah has with Him a great reward"* (8:28). The word is *fitnah*, a test; originally, the fire that tests gold. Wealth is named there not as a reward but as the thing a man is being tested with — which is very close to the question in this post's title.
</Claude>

So when is a gift not a gift? I think when it takes away more than it gives, and the man receiving it does not notice the taking. Money and power give a man room. What they take is quieter: the friction, the people who say no, the evenings at home, the version of himself that would have been embarrassed. A real gift leaves you more yourself than you were before. This kind can leave you a stranger to the people who knew you best, and the strange part is that all of it feels like success while it is happening.

What I take from all of this is small. The guardrails get built now, while nothing is at stake and nobody is watching -- in the small choices about where I spend my evenings, who I let close, and what I laugh along with.

<Claude re="The guardrails get built now">
Hamlet tells his mother how this works, and it is one of the most practical passages in Shakespeare: *"Assume a virtue, if you have it not. … Refrain tonight, / And that shall lend a kind of easiness / To the next abstinence; the next more easy; / For use almost can change the stamp of nature"* (Act III, scene 4). The guardrail is not one great decision. It is the next small refusal, which makes the one after it easier.

And George Eliot ends Middlemarch with the people this paragraph is about — the ones nobody watched: *"for the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs."*
</Claude>

There is one more thing about the stories we hand down. <Gloss on="A son does not inherit the story his father tells.">James Baldwin said it in one sentence: “Children have never been very good at listening to their elders, but they have never failed to imitate them” (“Fifth Avenue, Uptown”, 1960).</Gloss> <Gloss on="He inherits the one his father lives.">Child-development studies found something close to this. In experiments by James Bryan and colleagues in the late 1960s, children watched an adult who either preached generosity or practised it, and their own giving tended to follow what the adult did rather than what the adult said. A later study (Rushton, 1975) found that preaching did help over a longer stretch, so words are not useless. They just do not do the work alone.</Gloss> A father who speaks of loyalty and is careless with it has taught his son exactly one lesson, and it is not loyalty. Earlier I asked how we can take advice from people who are still kache. The harder question is whether our children will be taking it from us. If my life and my words do not match, then the half-hearted voice they grow up hearing is mine.

So the story I would want to hand down to our sons and daughters is one they can watch rather than one I have to tell: a measure that does not move with the room I am sitting in.
