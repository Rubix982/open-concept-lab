*Agar insaan ko pata hai ke kidher jana hai, kis taraf jana hai, to insaan deir kiyoun kare?*

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

اگر انسان کو پتا ہے کہ کدھر جانا ہے، کس طرف جانا ہے، تو انسان دیر کیوں کرے؟

</div>
</div>

<div className="ocl-bi">
<div className="ocl-bi__en">

If a person knows where to go, which direction to take, then why wait? Why waste time, and why not use the most of your potential?

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

اگر کسی کو معلوم ہو کہ کہاں جانا ہے، کس سمت جانا ہے، تو پھر انتظار کیوں؟ وقت کیوں ضائع کیا جائے، اور اپنی صلاحیت کا پورا فائدہ کیوں نہ اٹھایا جائے؟

</div>
</div>

<div className="ocl-bi">
<div className="ocl-bi__en">

If the path is long, and the time is limited, and we won't get to live again as we are living today, with the time we have, with the opportunities we have ... then we should make the most of it. Why delay? Why procrastinate?

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

اگر راستہ لمبا ہے، اور وقت محدود ہے، اور ہمیں دوبارہ اس طرح جینے کا موقع نہیں ملے گا جیسے آج جی رہے ہیں — اس وقت کے ساتھ جو ہمارے پاس ہے، ان مواقع کے ساتھ جو ہمارے پاس ہیں — تو ہمیں اس کا پورا فائدہ اٹھانا چاہیے۔ دیر کیوں؟ ٹال مٹول کیوں؟

</div>
</div>

<Claude re="we won't get to live again as we are living today">
Seneca wrote a whole essay on this, On the Shortness of Life, and it opens by turning the complaint around: *"It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it"* (1.3, in Basore's translation). Life is long enough, he says, if you use it; it only feels short to people who spent it waiting to begin.

Marcus Aurelius, writing notes to himself while running an empire, put it more bluntly: *"Do not act as if you were going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over you. While you live, while it is in your power, be good"* (Meditations 4.17).
</Claude>

<div className="ocl-bi">
<div className="ocl-bi__en">

It has a tonal shift, a kind of way, well, if you have some knowledge and some discipline, make use of it to do that business, to build that financial capital, to build the kind of life you have always wanted for yourself and your family.

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

اس میں لہجہ کچھ بدل جاتا ہے، کچھ یوں کہ: اگر تمہارے پاس کچھ علم ہے اور کچھ نظم و ضبط ہے، تو اسے کام میں لاؤ — وہ کاروبار کرنے کے لیے، وہ مالی سرمایہ بنانے کے لیے، وہ زندگی بنانے کے لیے جو تم نے ہمیشہ اپنے لیے اور اپنے گھر والوں کے لیے چاہی ہے۔

</div>
</div>

<div className="ocl-bi">
<div className="ocl-bi__en">

If you have ever wanted to make that thing, well, let's make that thing happen. Let's work towards it, let's push towards it -- <Gloss on="why let the hours slip by?">Hillel asked the same question two thousand years ago, at the end of three short ones: “If I am not for myself, who will be for me? And when I am for myself, what am I? And if not now, when?” (Pirkei Avot 1:14). And Annie Dillard, more quietly: “How we spend our days is, of course, how we spend our lives.”</Gloss> If you have ever wanted to fall in love, why wait to tell someone you love how you feel about them, why delay saying what is in our heart for the other person? Why delay telling our loved ones how much they matter to us and hesitate to live the life we have wanted to live for them, with them?

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

اگر تم نے کبھی وہ چیز بنانی چاہی ہے، تو چلو اسے کر دکھاتے ہیں۔ اس کی طرف کام کرتے ہیں، اس کی طرف زور لگاتے ہیں — گھنٹوں کو یوں ہی کیوں گزر جانے دیں؟ اگر تم نے کبھی محبت کرنی چاہی ہے، تو جس سے محبت ہے اسے دل کا حال بتانے میں دیر کیوں؟ دوسرے کے لیے جو ہمارے دل میں ہے، وہ کہنے میں تاخیر کیوں؟ اپنے پیاروں کو یہ بتانے میں دیر کیوں کہ وہ ہمارے لیے کتنے اہم ہیں، اور وہ زندگی جینے میں ہچکچاہٹ کیوں جو ہم ان کے لیے، ان کے ساتھ جینا چاہتے تھے؟

</div>
</div>

<Claude re="why delay saying what is in our heart">
Psychologists have studied which regrets stay with people, and this paragraph lands on the ones that last. Gilovich and Medvec (1995) found that in the short run people regret things they did more than things they didn't, but over a lifetime it reverses: the regrets that last are mostly the things never done — the word not said, the chance not taken. When people in their surveys looked back over their whole lives, the large majority of their biggest regrets were inactions. Kahneman pushed back that many of these regrets are wistful more than painful, and a later paper with him found both kinds exist. But neither side disputed which kind lasts longer.
</Claude>

<div className="ocl-bi">
<div className="ocl-bi__en">

If we have ever wanted to write down our deeper internal visions of reality and what we have experienced, we may not get another chance to do that. If we have ever wanted to kneel down in prayer and bow down and express what hurts us, why wait? Time is so little. We don't remember where we came from, we find it hard to explain where we are, and we for sure never know where we are going or what is waiting for us.

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

اگر ہم نے کبھی چاہا ہے کہ حقیقت کے بارے میں اپنے گہرے اندرونی خیالات، اور جو کچھ ہم پر گزرا ہے، لکھ ڈالیں، تو شاید ہمیں یہ موقع دوبارہ نہ ملے۔ اگر ہم نے کبھی چاہا ہے کہ نماز میں جھک جائیں، سجدے میں سر رکھ دیں، اور جو ہمیں دکھ دیتا ہے وہ کہہ دیں، تو انتظار کیوں؟ وقت بہت تھوڑا ہے۔ ہمیں یاد نہیں کہ ہم کہاں سے آئے، یہ بتانا مشکل لگتا ہے کہ ہم کہاں ہیں، اور یہ تو ہم یقیناً کبھی نہیں جانتے کہ ہم کہاں جا رہے ہیں، یا آگے کیا ہمارا منتظر ہے۔

</div>
</div>

<Claude re="Time is so little.">
There is a whole surah on this, one of the shortest in the Qur'an, and it begins by swearing by time itself: *"By time, indeed, mankind is in loss, except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience"* (103). Imam ash-Shafi'i is reported to have said that if people reflected on this surah alone, it would be enough for them.

The Prophet ﷺ said much the same in a list: *"Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before you become busy, and your life before your death"* (al-Hakim). And more briefly: *"There are two blessings which many people lose: health and free time"* (Bukhari). Free time is named as a blessing, which means it can be lost like one.
</Claude>

<div className="ocl-bi">
<div className="ocl-bi__en">

I can probably add a piece of Urdu poetry about how much we remember the lives we have lived and where we are now, or if we could go back and change things, how we could change it, but that is the thing, isn't it? That the life we said we would live, will never be lived if we choose to not live it. If we don't make the best of life as we thought we might make it, well, we will never get a second chance. So it hurts the same way that it fascinates us, but it also asks us to spend our time more responsibly and with care.

</div>
<div className="ocl-bi__ur" dir="rtl" lang="ur">

شاید میں یہاں اردو شاعری کا کوئی ٹکڑا شامل کر دوں — اس بارے میں کہ ہمیں اپنی گزری ہوئی زندگیاں کتنی یاد ہیں اور آج ہم کہاں ہیں، یا اگر ہم واپس جا کر چیزیں بدل سکتے تو کیسے بدلتے — مگر بات تو یہی ہے، ہے نا؟ کہ جو زندگی ہم نے کہا تھا کہ جئیں گے، اگر ہم اسے نہ جینا چن لیں تو وہ کبھی جی ہی نہیں جائے گی۔ اگر ہم زندگی کو ویسا نہ بنائیں جیسا ہم نے سوچا تھا، تو دوسرا موقع کبھی نہیں ملے گا۔ یہ بات جتنا دکھ دیتی ہے، اتنا ہی حیران بھی کرتی ہے، مگر یہ ہم سے یہ بھی مانگتی ہے کہ ہم اپنا وقت زیادہ ذمہ داری اور احتیاط سے گزاریں۔

</div>
</div>

<Claude re="I can probably add a piece of Urdu poetry">
Here is one that fits almost exactly:

*Umr-e-daraaz maang ke laai thi chaar din / Do aarzoo mein kat gaye, do intezaar mein.*

I asked for a long life, and was given four days. Two were spent in longing, and two in waiting. It is usually credited to Bahadur Shah Zafar, the last Mughal emperor, writing in exile, but it is not in any of his collections; Rekhta and others attribute it to Seemab Akbarabadi, who recited the ghazal at a mushaira in Aligarh in 1919. Whoever wrote it, it is your question turned into a complaint: the days did not run out because life was short. They ran out in longing and waiting.

And you already have the other one. The poem in [Unstructured Time](/writing/unstructured-time), Munir Niazi's *hamesha deir kar deta hoon main*, is about exactly this lateness. This post reads like its answer.
</Claude>

I guess it's just that -- <Gloss on="there is life after tomorrow for us">You wrote nearly this sentence the day before, in [When is a gift ... not a gift?](/writing/when-is-a-gift-not-a-gift), about young boys: that hardly any of them understand “there is a life after tomorrow”, one they will have to live. Here it comes back about yourself.</Gloss>, and I too have understood this too late -- tomorrow will come, and so will the day after, and the day after that. Our time is not worthless or insignificant or little, it is valuable and <Gloss on="really the only asset we have on our hands">Seneca opens his very first letter to Lucilius with this: “Omnia, Lucili, aliena sunt, tempus tantum nostrum est” — nothing, Lucilius, is ours except time (Letters 1.3). Everything else is on loan.</Gloss>.
