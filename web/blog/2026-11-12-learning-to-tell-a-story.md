How to tell a story? A good story. The kind of story that expresses a lot with little words. One where characters exchange glances and you can feel their inner chemistry -- one where you visualize what the characters look like from different angles, and how those angles communicate the inner working of your characters, and from the angle that they are looking at the world with.

<Claude re="exchange glances">
Film has a well-known demonstration of how little it takes. Around 1920 Lev Kuleshov cut one shot of an actor's neutral face against a bowl of soup, a child in a coffin, and a woman on a divan. Audiences, so the story goes, praised the actor's hunger, grief and desire, though the face never changed. The original reel is lost, and a 2016 replication (Barratt and colleagues) found the effect real but smaller than the legend. The part that holds up is the useful part here: the viewer supplies the feeling. A glance works because the reader fills it in, and they can only fill in what the story has already given them a reason to feel.
</Claude>

Perhaps the story you want to tell is a sad story. It makes you ponder on the bad moments in life, but in a fruitful manner so you take away some lesson there.

Perhaps the story you want to tell is a happy story. Something great happens to your character, and you want to tell viewers that many things are worth holding on to in life given enough time.

Perhaps the story you want to tell is a tragedy. The path that your person takes to avoid a fate is the same path that leads to that fate. <Gloss on="We become what we avoid.">La Fontaine has nearly this line in one of his fables, L'Horoscope: one often meets one's destiny on the road taken to avoid it. The oldest worked example is Oedipus, who leaves home to escape the prophecy and walks straight into it.</Gloss> We are the cause of our own undoing. A sadness through virtue.

<Claude re="A sadness through virtue">
Aristotle's Poetics is close to this, but not the same. He says the best tragic figure is neither wicked nor perfect, and falls through an error (hamartia), not through vice. Yours goes a step further: the fall comes through the good thing itself. Oedipus arguably fits your version better than Aristotle's, since what undoes him is his insistence on finding the truth to save the city. The difference is worth deciding for any story you write, because it changes what a reader takes away. A fall through error says be careful. A fall through virtue says some good things cost.
</Claude>

And perhaps you want to tell a deeper regret through framed story telling, in a mechanism of your choosing.

<Claude re="the story you want to tell">
The shapes in the figure have a history. Kurt Vonnegut drew stories as a line of good and bad fortune over time, and later said his master's thesis on the idea was rejected at Chicago for looking like too much fun. In 2016 Reagan and colleagues tested it by tracking the sentiment of 1,327 stories from Project Gutenberg. Most followed one of six arcs: a steady rise, a steady fall, fall then rise (Vonnegut's "man in a hole", your happy story), rise then fall, rise-fall-rise, and fall-rise-fall.

Drawing the figure showed one thing about your list. The sad and happy stories fit on that line, but the tragedy as you describe it doesn't. On a fortune line a tragic fall looks like any other fall. What makes it a tragedy is that the running caused it, and the line has no axis for cause. That is why it had to be drawn as a road and not a graph.
</Claude>

I have often wondered if this is how humanity is designed by The Divine -- humans are stories we tell each other through generation to generation, from father and mother to their sons and daughters, and these stories shape the kind of person we become,or at least show us the path we can choose to become respectively. I have wondered what a story might look like that inspires inner good in people.

Humans are flawed, and so are our stories. Without sadness and grief, it seems little reason for hope and striving to exist in contrast to. How would we compare the direction of what we are told if we could not foresee what our characters are going through?

And sometimes, looking back tatyour own life is itself a story. It is hard to believe you and I made it to where we are today through so many decades of our life, living the life that we live. We have experienced so much in this short frame of the universe, that we too, if we would be presented with our own life as a movie, be shocked to see just what we have been through that we would not wish on others.

I think story telling is a giant part of being a proper, well-rounded person. It teaches you how you frame life, what you take away from your experiences, and what experiences do we keep running into but don't pay attention to. In that light, I think story-telling is something to try and learn as well. Our story may mean little to us, but it can mean a lot to others, perhaps inspire some good, help someone overcome a blocker in their thoughts, perhaps see some perspectives that otherwise we could not see.

<Claude re="It teaches you how you frame life">
Psychology studies this under the name narrative identity. Dan McAdams and colleagues asked people to tell their life stories and coded the turning points. People whose stories had more redemption sequences, where something good comes out of a bad scene, tended to report higher well-being. People whose stories had more contamination sequences, where a good scene is spoiled, tended to report lower. The studies are correlational, so they can't say whether the framing helps or whether happier people simply tell it that way. It does fit your earlier paragraph about sadness giving hope something to contrast with: in a redemption sequence the good grows out of the bad scene, it doesn't replace it.
</Claude>

I think tech does this to people. At some point, I have started thinking if this is all my life is -- solving coding challenges, one after another -- but perhaps there is something more to people when it comes to the different experiences that we live through. There is a more desire to live life differently and to experience different things and actually own up the desire to go through challenges and difficulties that arrive to us and accept that as just how life is designed to be. Perhaps there is no story that starts well and is only well and ends well. Perhaps our search of a person for us that is not capable of hurting us, knowingly or unkwowingly, is also somewhat impossible. After all, we are human -- we hurt others despite our best of intentions. It doesn't mean that it doesn't pain us -- it does, but it comes with the part of being fragile and vulnerable as a human.

You can absolutely be rejected by the thing you are trying to get, and that rejection might obviously produce pain every single time despite the number of rejections, but a rejection passes, and life has to move on to someone or something that accepts the circumstances we are in and what we have the ability to offer as a person with our innate abilities.

So a story, fundamentally, is trying to communicate that flawed but innate desire of human awkwardness where we express what we feel and want deep down through the little things that we can do -- the way we look at someone, the way we hold ourselves and the other person's presence when we are near them, the way we talk and the way we carrry our words -- <Gloss on="the way we choose silence sometimes gives weight">Hemingway called this the iceberg: a writer can leave out what they know, and if the writing is true enough the reader will still feel it. His line was that the dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water (Death in the Afternoon, 1932).</Gloss> to whenever we speak more -- it is a dance we do with the people we meet so we can find a deeper bond, just so we can know the other person's story.
