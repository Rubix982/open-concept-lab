## What I was trying to do {#plain-what-i-was-trying-to-do}

You can reach into a language model and change one fact. *Maurice de Vlaminck was born in France* becomes *…in Chile*, and the whole thing takes seconds.

Then you have to ask what else moved, and that turns out to be the hard part. Nobody can write down everything a model believes, so the field does the practical thing and writes a list of test questions — paraphrases, related facts, questions a step or two removed — and checks those after the edit.

I kept getting stuck on the same objection. A list can tell you an edit broke something. It can never tell you an edit broke nothing, because a question nobody thought to ask and a failure that never happened produce the same thing: no result. Writing more questions does not fix it either; you are still measuring what the person writing them happened to imagine.

So I wanted a number instead of a list. Something you could compute for *any* question, including ones nobody wrote down.

This kind of edit turns out to have a shape that gives you one. Because of how it works, you can calculate how much of the edit lands on any given question — and you can do it before making the edit, from the untouched model, in a single pass. If that number were big wherever the damage was, you could point it at a question you had never thought about and get an answer.

## The part where I thought it worked {#plain-the-part-where-i-thought-it-worked}

I ran it against how much the edit actually moved the model on 495 questions. The correlation was **0.522**.

For about a day that was the result. It is a respectable number, it was in the direction I expected, and I had a story for why.

Then I ran the control, which was: edit a *different* fact about the same person, and see how much that moves the same questions. If the measure were really tracking the damage from this edit, that should move things much less.

It did not. Most of the correlation was already there. What is left once you take the control out is **0.085**, with a range of **−0.003 to 0.172** — a range that includes zero.

I sat with that for a while, because 0.522 and 0.085 are the same experiment and you can report either one. Once you account for *this question is about that person*, there is very little left that the measure was telling me.

## What the number is actually detecting {#plain-what-the-number-is-actually-detecting}

Looking at where it is large and where it is small made the problem obvious, and in hindsight it was obvious earlier.

On a question that *begins* with the edited name, it is exactly 1. That one is arithmetic rather than a measurement — it comes out of the algebra, and it does not depend on the model. Across questions that mention the name anywhere, the median is **0.9334**.

On questions that never mention him, it is **0.0543**.

That still looks like something, so I checked whether it is. Run a stranger's edit — someone else entirely — on the same questions, and they score **0.0481**. Almost the whole of the 0.0543 is just what any edit does to any question. The real edit beats a stranger by **0.8439** where the name is present and by **0.0002** where it is not.

So the number tells you whether the edited name is sitting in the question. Which is a thing you can already find out by reading the question.

And the case everybody means by *did the edit spread* is the one where the name is absent — you changed where he was born, does the model still say he was a painter? That question never says "Vlaminck". The measure has its full range in the case I did not need and essentially none in the case I did.

## Why there was so little there to find {#plain-why-there-was-so-little-there-to-find}

This bothered me more than the failure did, so I measured it separately on the same 495 questions.

Change what the edit *says* and keep the person: the questions move by **0.0009**. Change *whose* fact you edit and keep everything else: they move by **0.0583**. About sixty-three times more.

Most of what an edit does is a consequence of whose name you touched, and very little of it depends on what you said about them. That is why the correlation collapsed under a control that held the person fixed — there was not much edit-specific signal for any measure to find.

## What it turned out to be useful for {#plain-what-it-turned-out-to-be-useful-for}

If the number is high exactly when the edited name is in the question, then you can point it at a benchmark and ask which of its questions contain the name. That is not what I built it for, and it is the only thing it is good at.

RippleEdits has a test type meant to check whether an edit travels two steps — from the edited person, to where he died, to who governs that place. Its questions read *"the name of the head of government of the place of death of **Leo Arons** is…"*. The edited name is in the question. Those items take a median **0.982** of the update, against a floor of **0.065** from a stranger's edit on the same questions. An item like that can be answered by the edit arriving at the name directly, so a method could score well on it while having generalised nothing.

It is a fault in particular items, and it can be found. In MQuAKE the name is present in **6,861 of 6,861** items where the edit sits at the start of the chain, and in **0 of 16,506** where it sits further down — so a clean subset already exists in the field's own data, by construction rather than by anyone's design.

## What I am not saying {#plain-what-i-am-not-saying}

- Not that edits do not spread. Only that this measure cannot see whether they do.
- Not that content is irrelevant. It is detectable at **0.0009** and that interval excludes zero — roughly 1.5% of the effect of identity, which is small and is not nothing.
- Not that RippleEdits is wrong. One of its test types can be answered the easy way.
- The one universal upper limit this shape allows turns out to permit a question that never names the subject to receive *more* than one that does, so it cannot tell those two questions apart and rules nothing out.
- All of this is one editing method at one layer. The piece of arithmetic underneath it was checked on three models, each at four different depths, and came out exactly right all twelve times. The comparison value it is read against did not hold still at all — it moved by a factor of twelve across those same readings, so it has to be measured on whichever model you are actually using. None of this is a claim about every way of changing a model's mind.

## The part I got wrong first {#plain-the-part-i-got-wrong-first}

The control that decides everything above was wrong in its first version, and the wrong version made the result look better than it was. I found it, fixed it, and the paper keeps the broken one on the page with what changed.

The control is the whole argument. Without it, 0.522 stands and this reads as a success.
