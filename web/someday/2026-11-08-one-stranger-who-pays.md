Lately I've been thinking about problems from a business angle, and about the people who would pay for the answer more than the answer itself. A paying customer is the most honest test of whether something is needed. It is the same test I ran on the security ideas this week, where most died because someone already sells them or no one would use them. Money is the strictest version of that test.

**What it is not, yet.** A product idea. Every idea that died this week started from a product, a feature list or something that sounded clever. The ones that work tend to start from a person who already spends time or money on a problem and handles it badly.

**The first milestone.** One stranger paying me for something. Not a lot of money, not a company. Small and dull is fine; Dependabot was a small bootstrapped business for a long time before GitHub bought it. That one event would teach me more than months of thinking about it.

**The filters, from Wednesday:**

- Is it actually needed, by someone I can name?
- Is it too small or awkward for funded companies to bother with?
- Can I keep doing it alone?

**The tension I should decide on purpose.** My own Manifesto says I spend my time on the layer that is not yet solved, not the layer that already pays. A business is the layer that pays. That doesn't make this wrong. A business can fund the research, or stay a side build. But the two compete for the same weekends, and if I drift into this without deciding, I'll feel guilty about whichever one I'm not doing. It's worth one sitting to say which is the main climb for the next six months.

**Before charging anyone.** Check what my visa and my job contract allow. Building something and giving it away is usually fine; taking money is where the risk starts. Ask a lawyer before the first paid customer, not after.

**First step.** A list of five people I could actually talk to this month about a problem they already have. No product in the conversation.
